Introduction

SAP Ariba Buying is a next-generation, cloud-based procurement solution positioned as a suite-first, ERP-integrated, AI-enabled offering serving a large enterprise customer base. This Design Excellence Review examined the live product across two core scenarios — catalog and non-catalog procurement — covering the SAP Ariba Landing page, shopping and search, item comparison, cart and request management, approvals, notifications, goods receipt, and track-and-trace. The product demonstrates clear depth of functionality and meaningful procurement-specific intelligence, but the next generation is not yet at feature parity with the current generation, and the experience reveals recurring frictions around terminology, status visualization, contextual affordances, and information prominence. Overall, the UX maturity is mid-stage: the foundations and workflows are in place and competitive on capability, but the surface experience still trails suite competitors like Coupa on clarity, polish, and user-centered defaults — gaps that, if left unaddressed, will continue to weigh on adoption of the next-gen product against more usability-forward alternatives.

Strengths
  • Draft cart support enables professional buyers to manage multiple parallel purchases, with the ability to copy previous requisitions for repeat orders.
  • The item comparison view keeps the product image visible while scrolling and highlights rows with differences in blue, making cross-item evaluation easier.
  • Standard Fiori paging with previous/next item alt-text supports efficient navigation through objects.
  • Search flexibility is strong, allowing users to find requests by purchase order number as well as by code or name.
  • Admin-configurable email notifications across stages and failures, combined with planned document flow for end-to-end requisition-to-invoice traceability, reflect a thoughtful approach to procurement visibility.
Areas of Improvement
  • Inconsistent and ambiguous terminology: Terms such as Checkout, Save Cart, Submit Request, Back to Request, Composing, Request, and Requisition appear across the flow without clear differentiation, mixing status language with action language and risking user confusion at decision points that matter (e.g., abandoning a purchase or misunderstanding autosave).
  • Status visualization and affordance clarity: Status treatments are inconsistent and at times misleading — a green check appears at step 2 of 5 because the progress bar component cannot suppress it; some statuses carry icons while others do not; and color choices (e.g., blue for Partially Fulfilled) and the use of the info icon raise questions about whether states are neutral or action-needed. This is compounded by an active effort to standardize on a pill pattern across the Procurement Suite, which the casual-user progress bar diverges from.
  • Discoverability and prominence of key actions and information: Important entry points and information are buried — non-catalog ordering is nestled under admin-configured Quick Links, the Needs Attention tab is secondary to a 117-item full list, the persistent welcome banner consumes prime real estate, and the Need-By Date cannot be edited contextually from the cart summary. Users must hunt for what should be front-and-center.
  • Mental model misalignment between page context and content: Page headers, navigation outcomes, and component behaviors do not consistently match user intent — clicking Shopping lands users on a Search Results page with no query, the cart name field does not read as editable, autosave occurs without UI feedback, and item header rows display ##1, ##2, ##3 numbering that adds clutter without meaning.
  • Insufficient contextual guidance and proactive insight: Users are exposed to fields and decisions (e.g., goods receipt requirement, commodity codes, comment visibility) without field-level help or audience cues, and the system does not proactively notify requesters when delivery is at risk of missing the Need-By date, leaving them to discover problems after the fact.
Recommendations
  • Conduct a terminology and content audit across the buying flow — aligning Request vs. Requisition, Checkout vs. Submit vs. Save Cart, and Composing vs. Edit — and codify the outcomes with the Content (UX Language) team to ensure consistent, role-appropriate wording.
  • Partner with the Fiori Design System team to resolve the progress bar check-icon constraint and to reconcile the casual-user progress visualization with the Procurement Suite pill standard, then apply consistent icon and color rules across all status entries.
  • Re-prioritize the landing page and request list information architecture: elevate non-catalog entry, make Needs Attention the primary view where appropriate, dismiss the welcome banner once seen, and enable inline editing of Need-By Date from the cart summary.
  • Introduce contextual feedback and guidance throughout editing flows — autosave confirmations, clear ownership of comment visibility (internal vs. supplier), editable-field affordances on cart name, and field-level help for procurement concepts such as goods receipt and commodity codes.
  • Treat proactive risk notifications (e.g., Need-By date at risk due to shipment or approval delays) and partial-receipt-to-full-receipt conversion as MVP rather than roadmap, given their direct impact on next-gen adoption parity with the current generation.
